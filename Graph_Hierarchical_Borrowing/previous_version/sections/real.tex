\section{Real Hospital Networks}\label{sec:real}

The simulations control everything; real data control nothing. This section asks whether GHB's advantages survive real hospitals, real heterogeneity and real sample sizes, against the federated and personalized methods that apply, at the same privacy budget. It reports four studies. The first is a confirmation on networks that the development of GHB's final version did not use. Its rule, its predictions and the frozen configuration of every method were committed to the repository before it ran (Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}). The second is the earlier benchmark of eight smaller networks, on which GHB-lite was developed and its limits were found (Section~\ref{sec:real-eight}). The third is a further pre-registered test, on the public FLamby benchmark \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby}, whose predictions for an earlier version failed, and which then served, with the eight networks, to develop the final version (Section~\ref{sec:real-flamby}). The design of GHB in Section~\ref{sec:logit} came out of the limits found on the eight networks, and every step of that development is reported, including the four pre-registered attempts that did not deliver: two failed their own criteria, the third held its predictions but showed that its extensions did not help, and the fourth, on FLamby \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby}, failed three of its four predictions. The fourth, on images, extends GHB with a frozen public encoder and confirms it on histopathology under a further pre-registration (Section~\ref{sec:real-images}).

\subsection{A pre-registered test on unseen networks}\label{sec:real-tcga}

The strongest test a method can face is a single run on data that played no part in its design, with its predictions written down beforehand. This subsection reports such a test: the networks, the protocol, how GHB was developed and frozen, the outcome against each prediction, and what changes when GHB is tuned like its competitors.

\paragraph{Networks and model} The Cancer Genome Atlas \cite{weinstein2013cancer,grossman2016toward} records, for every patient, the institution that contributed the tissue. Each cancer type is a network, and each institution with at least five eligible patients is a hospital. The outcome is death within two years of diagnosis; patients alive with less than two years of follow-up are excluded. Every hospital fits a logistic regression with eight coefficients: intercept, age, sex, pathologic stage III, stage IV, tumour stage T3 or T4, nodal involvement and a prior malignancy, with $\|x\|\le\sqrt{8}$ by construction (FIGO stage replaces the AJCC stage for the gynecologic cancers). Twelve networks served for development: lung squamous and adenocarcinoma, head and neck, bladder, stomach, liver, clear-cell and papillary kidney, colon, rectum, pancreas and oesophagus (5 to 17 hospitals each). The confirmation set was then fixed by a rule on counts alone, before any model was fitted to it. It contains every further TCGA project with at least four such hospitals and at least fifteen deaths: breast (8 hospitals), mesothelioma (9), ovarian (15), endometrial (15) and uterine carcinosarcoma (5). Hospitals hold 5 to 109 eligible patients.

\paragraph{Protocol} Every hospital is evaluated on its own held-out patients by ten repetitions of stratified five-fold cross-validation, and errors are held-out log-loss relative to local estimation under the default prior $\mathcal{N}(0,2.5^2)$. Every private method spends the same budget, $\epsilon\in\{1,10\}$ with $\delta=10^{-5}$ under zero-concentrated accounting. The literature methods are DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with local fine-tuning, with and without secure aggregation \cite{bonawitz2017practical}; clustered DP-FedAvg \cite{ghosh2020efficient}; APFL \cite{deng2020adaptive}; Ditto \cite{li2021ditto}; and pFedMe \cite{tdinh2020personalized}. Each has a grid of 12 to 54 configurations, and how it is tuned decides how it compares. pFedMe releases the change of a proximal fit that minimizes the sum of a hospital's losses plus $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta-w\|^2$; one patient moves that fit by up to $2G/\lambda$, so $S$ local steps of size $\beta$ have sensitivity $S\beta\,2G/\lambda$. An earlier version of this analysis used $S\beta\,2G/(n\lambda)$, $n$ times too small, which made pFedMe look strong; every pFedMe result reported here uses the correct calibration, and every other method is unaffected. In the \emph{realistic} arm, each method uses the configuration with the lowest mean error over the twelve development networks, frozen before the confirmation. This is what a consortium could do with networks it already has. In the \emph{oracle} arm, each method uses the configuration that is best on the confirmation network's own held-out folds, which no hospital can do. GHB is not tuned: its configuration (Section~\ref{sec:logit}) was chosen among about fifteen variants on the development networks and frozen together with the baselines'. The two privacy budgets bracket the regime of interest. At $\epsilon=1$ the joint releases of a network of a few hundred patients are noisy. At $\epsilon=10$ they are accurate, and borrowing can pay.

\paragraph{How GHB was developed} GHB-lite, with an evidence-weighted default prior, was the starting point. On the twelve development networks it is no better than a local model with the same prior at $\epsilon=1$ (\SimTDOneLite{} against \SimTDOneLoc), and worse than it at $\epsilon=10$ (\SimTDTenLite{} against \SimTDTenLoc). A pre-registered extension of GHB-lite added four components: a no-borrowing option, a network-learned prior scale, correlated heterogeneity and borrowing across cancer types. It was confirmed on six networks that were then unseen. Its predictions held as recorded (with pFedMe's noise corrected, the part of one prediction concerning pFedMe no longer holds; supplement), but at $\epsilon=10$ every variant was worse than local estimation, which the extension was meant to prevent; that confirmation made those six networks part of the development set. The diagnosis was the bias and the noise of GHB-lite's per-hospital releases (Section~\ref{sec:logit}), and GHB replaces them by the two joint releases. Its first version (GHB v4) was frozen and confirmed on the five networks below in a first pre-registered test, in which all three of its predictions held, but under hindsight tuning at $\epsilon=10$ two literature methods were slightly more accurate (supplement). A second round of development then used the twelve development networks, the eight smaller networks of Section~\ref{sec:real-eight} and the FLamby benchmark \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby} of Section~\ref{sec:real-flamby}, and never the five confirmation networks. It added the three changes of Section~\ref{sec:logit}: the intercept is not borrowed, the information release has its tighter sensitivity, and it falls back to its diagonal by a public rule. It rejected two others: weighting the components by leave-one-out predictive accuracy, which helped one network and hurt the others, and a noise-aware network estimate, which hurt at $\epsilon=10$. On the development networks GHB reaches \SimTDOneGHB{} at $\epsilon=1$ and \SimTDTenGHB{} at $\epsilon=10$, against \SimTDOneVFour{} and \SimTDTenVFour{} for its first version and \SimTDOneBestLit{} and \SimTDTenBestLit{} for the best literature method tuned on the same networks. The released configuration was frozen and its predictions committed before it ran on the five confirmation networks. Its components were developed without them, but the first version's results on them were known, so this is a test on networks unseen by the development of what changed, not on data never looked at.

\paragraph{Predictions and outcome} The pre-registration made five predictions; four held and one failed (Table~\ref{tab:tcga-confirm}).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{GHB has a lower mean error than every literature method in the realistic arm, at both budgets.} Held. At $\epsilon=1$, GHB reaches \SimTCOneGHB{}, against \SimTCOneFT{} for the best of them, DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning, between \SimTCOneClu{} and \SimTCOneDitto{} for four of the others, and \SimTCOnePFedMe{} for pFedMe \cite{tdinh2020personalized}, whose noise at this budget swamps its updates (see the protocol above). At $\epsilon=10$, GHB reaches \SimTCTenGHB{}, against \SimTCTenDitto{} for Ditto \cite{li2021ditto}; the other five reach between \SimTCTenFT{} and \SimTCTenPFedMe.
\item \emph{GHB is more accurate than its first version at both budgets, with the same privacy noise.} Held: \SimTCOneGHB{} against \SimTCOneVFourPaired{} at $\epsilon=1$, and \SimTCTenGHB{} against \SimTCTenVFourPaired{} at $\epsilon=10$, for the first version rerun with the same privacy noise as the released one (the table's row for the first version is its original pre-registered run).
\item \emph{At $\epsilon=10$ GHB beats Ditto in at least four of the five networks.} Failed: it beats Ditto \cite{li2021ditto} in \SimTCTenDittoWins{} of the five, losing on mesothelioma and uterine carcinosarcoma, the two networks with the fewest patients.
\item \emph{At $\epsilon=1$ GHB is more accurate than every literature method tuned on the test folds.} Held: the best of them, DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning, reaches \SimTCOneFTOrc, and even the best method chosen afresh for each network reaches only \SimTCOneBestOrc.
\item \emph{At $\epsilon=10$ GHB is more accurate than every literature method tuned on the test folds.} Held, narrowly: Ditto \cite{li2021ditto} reaches \SimTCTenDittoOrc{} and DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with secure aggregation \SimTCTenFTSAOrc. Only the best method chosen afresh for each network with hindsight is more accurate (\SimTCTenBestOrc). The first version failed this comparison.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Tuning} GHB has nothing to tune, and untuned it is already ahead of every literature method tuned in either way. Its first version was also tuned like the baselines, over 36 configurations of its prior scale, the weight of its non-borrowing components and the split of its budget; tuning changed it by a few thousandths (supplement). GHB's lead, in other words, does not come from leaving its competitors untuned.

\paragraph{What the confirmation supports} Five networks are a small sample. At $\epsilon=10$ the margins are small, \SimTCTenDitto{} for Ditto \cite{li2021ditto} against \SimTCTenGHB{} with three wins in five, and a different set of networks could reverse them. The claim the confirmation supports is therefore specific. Without any tuning, on networks that the development of its final components did not use, GHB was more accurate than every federated and personalized method, tuned in the way a consortium could tune it or on the test patients themselves, at both budgets. Only the best of the six methods chosen afresh for every network with hindsight was more accurate, at $\epsilon=10$. It does not support the claim that GHB dominates the literature in every network.

\subsection{Eight smaller networks}\label{sec:real-eight}

GHB-lite was developed on the eight networks below, and its limits were found on them. GHB was added afterwards, with its configuration frozen as in Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga} and nothing else changed, so every other number is as in the runs without it. Two of these networks, Arizona Medicare and UCI Heart Disease, played no part in the development of GHB's first version; the development of the released version used all eight (Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}). The six TCGA networks here use a simpler head than in Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}, and each network is fitted on its own, so GHB cannot borrow across cancer types.

\paragraph{Eight real networks} All data are public and need no credentials. \emph{Arizona Medicare} \cite{hilbe2011negative}: 1,466 admissions for percutaneous cardiovascular procedures at 43 hospitals with at least five patients (median 29), in-hospital death (34\%), predictors age 80 or over, HMO membership, urgent and emergency admission. \emph{The Cancer Genome Atlas} \cite{weinstein2013cancer,grossman2016toward}: six networks, one per cancer type (lung squamous, head and neck, bladder, stomach, liver and clear-cell kidney carcinoma), each hospital the tissue source site that contributed its patients (7 to 17 hospitals, median 11 to 29 patients), death within two years of diagnosis, predictors age, sex, and pathologic stage III and IV. \emph{UCI Heart Disease} \cite{detrano1989international}: the four hospitals described below, used as the development network. With four or five coefficients and a few dozen patients per hospital these networks hold little to share, which is the setting of siloed smallness and also the hardest one for any collaborative method.

\paragraph{Protocol} Every hospital is evaluated on its own held-out patients by repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation (100 splits per network), and errors are reported as held-out log-loss relative to local estimation with the default prior. Every private method spends the same budget per hospital, $\epsilon\in\{1,10\}$ and $\delta=10^{-5}$, with zero-concentrated accounting. GHB and GHB-lite run without an anchor, because no published model is assumed; GHB-lite inverts its releases with a nominal public feature distribution fixed before the data were seen, which GHB does not need. Seven networks are evaluated: Arizona Medicare and the six TCGA networks. UCI Heart Disease, the eighth, served for development and enters only the tuning of the baselines. Twelve literature methods are compared: DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} alone; DP-FedAvg with local fine-tuning, with and without secure aggregation \cite{bonawitz2017practical}; decentralized DP-SGD \cite{lian2017can}; clustered DP-FedAvg \cite{ghosh2020efficient}; a one-shot Ditto-style model \cite{li2021ditto}; and the personalized methods Ditto \cite{li2021ditto}, pFedMe \cite{tdinh2020personalized}, APFL \cite{deng2020adaptive}, Per-FedAvg \cite{fallah2020personalized}, FedRep \cite{collins2021exploiting} and FedProx \cite{li2020federated}, each with private gradients under the same accounting (SCAFFOLD \cite{karimireddy2020scaffold} and FedProx with one full-batch local step coincide with DP-FedAvg). Each has a grid of hyperparameters, and how it is tuned decides how it compares. Three tuning arms are reported. \emph{Oracle}: tuned on each network's own held-out folds, an upper bound no hospital can reach. \emph{Leave-one-network-out}: the configuration with the lowest mean error on the other seven networks, the most generous tuning a hospital could actually do. \emph{Development}: tuned on UCI Heart Disease alone and frozen. GHB and GHB-lite with the evidence-weighted prior (GHB-lite-EB) have nothing to tune. Two local-only references separate what collaboration contributes from what regularization does: local estimation with a tuned prior scale, and local gradient descent with a tuned number of steps.

\paragraph{With strong privacy, collaboration adds little for anyone} At $\epsilon=1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:real-final}, Table~\ref{tab:real-final}) GHB reduces the error of local estimation to \SimRFOneV{} on average (\SimRFOneVMin{} to \SimRFOneVMax{} across networks). Tuned on the other networks, no literature method does better on average. Ditto \cite{li2021ditto} comes closest (\SimRFOneDittoLono), and GHB beats each of the twelve methods in at least six of the seven networks. Tuned on each network's own test folds, DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning and secure aggregation reaches \SimRFOneFTSAOrc. But at this budget a single hospital that regularizes well does as well as any collaborative method: local estimation with a tuned prior reaches \SimRFOneLocLono, and GHB-lite-EB \SimRFOneEB. These networks hold a few hundred patients each (Medicare about 1,500), and noise calibrated to that total still swamps what a head of four or five coefficients could borrow. The privacy noise of each per-hospital release is many times the hospital's own sampling error (Section~\ref{sec:heart-size} below), so GHB-lite falls back to regularization.

\paragraph{With weaker privacy, GHB leads every method a consortium could tune} At $\epsilon=10$, collaboration starts to pay. GHB (\SimRFTenV) is more accurate than local estimation with a tuned prior in \SimRFTenVvsLocWins{} of the seven networks. Tuned on the other networks, no literature method is more accurate on average: the closest are DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning and secure aggregation (\SimRFTenFTSALono) and Ditto \cite{li2021ditto} (\SimRFTenDittoLono), and the other ten are less accurate. GHB is more accurate than each of them in at least five of the seven networks. Tuned on the test folds, DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning and secure aggregation leads by less than one percent (\SimRFTenFTSAOrc). GHB's worst hospital is the liver network's hospital whose 22 patients all died, where every method that borrows fails (\SimRFTenLiverWorstV{} for GHB). GHB-lite-EB reaches \SimRFTenEB, a smaller gain than GHB's. These networks are fitted one at a time, so GHB cannot borrow across cancer types, which the ablation found to be one of its two most important components.

\paragraph{Tuning is most of the baselines' advantage} The comparison turns on how the literature methods are tuned, and GHB has nothing to tune. Every baseline loses accuracy when its settings must come from other networks, and several become unstable. Tuned on UCI Heart Disease alone, the mean error at $\epsilon=1$ becomes \SimRFOneFTSAUci{} for DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning and secure aggregation and \SimRFOneDittoUci{} for Ditto \cite{li2021ditto}, against \SimRFOneV{} for GHB, and FedRep \cite{collins2021exploiting} diverges. pFedMe \cite{tdinh2020personalized} fails at $\epsilon=1$ under every tuning (\SimRFOnePFedMeOrc{} even in the oracle arm): with its noise correctly calibrated, its updates are mostly noise. A small hospital cannot tune on its own test patients, and has few patients to hold out for validation. The leave-one-network-out arm is the most a consortium could do for it.

\paragraph{The road to GHB} The first real-data run placed GHB-lite at the local level (\SimRFOneGHB{} at $\epsilon=1$): its fixed default prior $\mathcal{N}(0,2.5^2)$ is too loose for hospitals of a few dozen patients. Two improvements were then pre-registered on these networks, and both failed their criteria; the supplement gives the full record. Integrating the scale of the default prior out by each hospital's evidence (GHB-lite-EB) improved on GHB-lite in \SimRFOneEBvsGHBWins{} of the seven networks, but raised the worst-hospital ratio in the liver network from \SimRFOneLiverWorstGHB{} to \SimRFOneLiverWorstEB. Its gain equals that of a local model with the same prior, so it comes from regularization, not from borrowing. Secure aggregation of per-hospital quantities, still inverted under a public feature distribution, improved on GHB-lite-EB in only \SimRFOneSAvsEBWins{} of seven. The lesson that led to GHB is in what neither changed: as long as the network estimate is read through a public feature distribution and per-hospital noise, it carries too little information to beat a well-regularized hospital.

\subsubsection{How much the size of a hospital matters}\label{sec:heart-size} Why per-hospital releases carry so little is clearest in the smallest network, UCI Heart Disease, where the size of each hospital can be varied by subsampling. This analysis concerns GHB-lite and the baselines it was first compared with, tuned on the held-out folds; it explains the limit that GHB was built to remove. The UCI Heart Disease data \cite{detrano1989international} are the classic multi-site benchmark of federated learning in healthcare \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby}. They hold the records of patients referred for coronary angiography at four hospitals: the Cleveland Clinic (303 patients), the Hungarian Institute of Cardiology in Budapest (294), the University Hospitals of Zurich and Basel (123) and the VA Medical Center in Long Beach (200). The prevalence of angiographic coronary disease ranges from 36\% in Budapest to 93\% in Zurich and Basel, so the heterogeneity is real and large. Each hospital fits the pretest-probability model of coronary disease, a logistic regression on age, sex and chest-pain type (asymptomatic, anginal, or non-anginal as reference), the predictors that every site records completely. Age is scaled by public constants, $(\text{age}-55)/25$ clipped to $[-1,1]$, so that $\|x\|\le\sqrt{5}$ without fitting anything to the data. No published model is assumed, so GHB-lite runs without an anchor: it releases the score at the public point 0, linearizes under the default prior, and gossips on a ring of four hospitals. The public feature distribution needed for the inversion is a nominal chest-pain clinic population (age $\mathcal{N}(55,9^2)$, 75\% men, 50\% asymptomatic, 30\% anginal pain), not the data, so every hospital is under a real case-mix shift. Evaluation is by repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation within each hospital (\SimRealHeartSplits{} splits). Each hospital trains on its training folds, subsampled to at most 20, 40 or 80 patients to emulate siloed smallness, or on all of them, and is scored by log-loss on its own held-out fold. The baselines are the same as in Simulation 15, with their hyperparameters chosen on the held-out folds themselves, which favours them. A local-only control, gradient descent on the hospital's own data with a tuned number of steps, separates what they gain by collaborating from what they gain by regularizing.

The result is mixed, and it is consistent with the theory. At $\epsilon=1$, no private method gains from collaboration in this network. GHB-lite stays within 1\% of local estimation at every training size (\SimRealHeartTwentyGHBRel{} with at most 20 patients, \SimRealHeartAllGHBRel{} with all of them), and its worst hospital stays within a factor \SimRealHeartGHBWorstMax{} of its local error, at $\epsilon=5$ too. The privacy noise explains why. On the parameter scale, a hospital of 40 patients releases noise with a standard deviation about 26 times its own sampling error at $\epsilon=1$, and about 10 times at 300 patients. With only three other hospitals to average over, $M$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:M} dwarfs $\tau^2$ and Equation~\eqref{eq:borrowed} leaves almost nothing to borrow. GHB-lite's posterior over $\tau$ and the hospital's own evidence detect this and fall back to local estimation, without any tuning. The tuned DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning appears to gain (\SimRealHeartTwentyFTRel{} with 20 patients). The local-only control gains exactly as much (\SimRealHeartTwentyLGDRel), because the chosen configuration is a single private round followed by 30 local steps, so its gain is early stopping tuned on the test folds, not borrowing. The Ditto-style \cite{li2021ditto} model, pulled toward the average of the private releases, fails badly with small hospitals (\SimRealHeartTwentyDittoRel). Without privacy, GHB-lite borrows: \SimRealHeartTwentyGHBNoDPRel{} of the local error with at most 20 training patients, \SimRealHeartFortyGHBNoDPRel{} with 40 and \SimRealHeartEightyGHBNoDPRel{} with 80, fading to \SimRealHeartAllGHBNoDPRel{} with all of them, as Equation~\eqref{eq:borrowed} predicts when $n_k$ grows. The nominal public feature distribution costs nothing: replacing it by the pooled empirical features gives \SimRealHeartTwentyGHBEmpRel{} with 20 patients. A single pooled model, also without privacy, is worse than local estimation on average at every size (\SimRealHeartTwentyGlobalRel{} to \SimRealHeartAllGlobalRel) and makes the Swiss hospital, with its 93\% prevalence, \SimRealHeartGlobalWorstMin{} to \SimRealHeartGlobalWorstMax{} times worse off. GHB-lite without privacy keeps every hospital within 2\% of its local error. The lesson for deployment is the one the theory states. In a network of four hospitals of tens to a few hundred patients, a guarantee of $\epsilon=1$ per release leaves nothing to share, and GHB-lite's value there is that it does no harm. Collaboration under privacy needs more hospitals (Simulation 17), releases that are calibrated to the whole network rather than to each hospital, which is what GHB does (Section~\ref{sec:logit}), or a larger budget.

\subsection{A public benchmark: FLamby}\label{sec:real-flamby}

The confirmation of Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga} used networks built from one data source by us. A method meant for other people's networks should also face a benchmark built by others. FLamby \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby} is the standard cross-silo benchmark of federated learning in healthcare, with natural hospital splits and fixed train and test sets. We ran the first version of GHB (v4) on it under a second pre-registration, again committed before any model was fitted, with every configuration frozen as in Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}. Its outcome then served as development data for the released version, whose results on FLamby \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby} are reported after the pre-registered ones and are development results, not a test.

\paragraph{Datasets and protocol} \emph{Fed-Heart-Disease} has the four hospitals of UCI Heart Disease with FLamby's preprocessing (13 features) and split; the head is a logistic regression, and FLamby's metric is accuracy, to which we add held-out log-loss. \emph{Fed-TCGA-BRCA} has six regions of breast-cancer patients with 39 features and a survival outcome, evaluated by the concordance index. GHB extends to it unchanged in structure: an exponential survival model is a Poisson regression whose likelihood is a sum over patients, so the two joint releases keep the guarantee of Proposition~\ref{prop:ghbprivacy} with the per-patient score $x(d-t)$ and information $t\,xx^\top$ at the origin; the literature methods clip each patient's gradient to the same bound. These patients overlap with the breast-cancer network of Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}, grouped there by institution and here by region, so this dataset is not unseen in the same sense. Each method was run 20 times at $\epsilon\in\{1,10\}$ and without privacy; literature methods use the configurations frozen on the twelve development networks (realistic arm) or the best on the test sets (oracle). GHB-lite was not run on TCGA-BRCA, because its inversion needs a public model of follow-up and censoring that does not exist.

\paragraph{Predictions and outcome} The pre-registration made three testable predictions about the first version, and none held (Tables~\ref{tab:flamby-heart} and~\ref{tab:flamby-brca}, rows GHB v4). A fourth, on the image dataset Fed-ISIC2019 (a frozen public encoder with eight one-vs-rest heads), held, but only because every method that borrows stayed at the level of a hospital alone (balanced accuracy about 0.30; supplement).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{On Fed-Heart-Disease at $\epsilon=1$, no private method improves on local log-loss by more than 3\%.} It failed in GHB's favour: GHB v4 improved on local estimation by \SimFHeartOneGHBGain{} and Ditto \cite{li2021ditto} by \SimFHeartOneDittoGain. Its gain equals that of a local model with the evidence-weighted prior (\SimFHeartLocalEB), so it comes from regularization, as on the small networks of Section~\ref{sec:real-eight}.
\item \emph{On Fed-Heart-Disease at $\epsilon=10$ and without privacy, GHB has a lower log-loss than every literature method.} It failed: DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning and secure aggregation (\SimFHeartTenFTSA{} and \SimFHeartInfFTSA) and Ditto (\SimFHeartTenDitto{} and \SimFHeartInfDitto) were more accurate than GHB v4 (\SimFHeartTenGHB{} and \SimFHeartInfGHB), which beat the other four. GHB-lite was more accurate than GHB v4 on this dataset (\SimFHeartTenLite{} and \SimFHeartInfLite).
\item \emph{On Fed-TCGA-BRCA at $\epsilon=10$ and without privacy, GHB's concordance is at least that of every literature method.} It failed: GHB v4 improved strongly on local estimation (\SimFBrcaTenGHB{} and \SimFBrcaInfGHB, against \SimFBrcaLocal), but DP-FedAvg \cite{mcmahan2018learning} with fine-tuning and secure aggregation was better at $\epsilon=10$ (\SimFBrcaTenFTSA) and pFedMe \cite{tdinh2020personalized} without privacy (\SimFBrcaInfPFedMe). With its privacy noise correctly calibrated (Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}), pFedMe falls to \SimFBrcaTenPFedMe{} at $\epsilon=10$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The released version on FLamby \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby} (development)} Two of the changes of the released version came from this benchmark: not borrowing the intercept, prompted by Fed-Heart-Disease, whose hospitals' prevalence ranges from 36\% to 93\%, and the diagonal fallback, prompted by the 40 coefficients of Fed-TCGA-BRCA. On Fed-TCGA-BRCA it is the most accurate method at $\epsilon=1$ by a wide margin (concordance \SimFBrcaOneVFive, against \SimFBrcaOneFTSA{} for the best literature method), and level with the best without privacy (\SimFBrcaInfVFive{} against \SimFBrcaInfPFedMe{} for pFedMe); at $\epsilon=10$ DP-FedAvg with fine-tuning and secure aggregation remains more accurate (\SimFBrcaTenFTSA{} against \SimFBrcaTenVFive). On Fed-Heart-Disease it has the lowest log-loss of every federated and personalized method without privacy (\SimFHeartInfVFive), is between DP-FedAvg with secure aggregation (\SimFHeartTenFTSA) and Ditto (\SimFHeartTenDitto) at $\epsilon=10$ (\SimFHeartTenVFive), and slightly behind Ditto at $\epsilon=1$ (\SimFHeartOneVFive{} against \SimFHeartOneDitto); GHB-lite stays more accurate without privacy. On Fed-ISIC2019, with the tabular head on a random projection of the image features, every method that borrows, the released version included, stays at the level of a hospital alone; the image version of Section~\ref{sec:real-images} was developed from this failure. Because the benchmark shaped these changes, these numbers show what the released version does on it, not that its lead would carry over to unseen benchmarks.

\paragraph{What the benchmark shows} Three features of FLamby \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby} plausibly explain the outcome of the pre-registered test, although none was tested in advance. Its networks are small, four to six hospitals of a few hundred patients, so the two joint releases carry less than on the cancer networks of Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}, which GHB fitted jointly and could borrow across. FLamby \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby} standardizes the features within each hospital, which makes the public feature distribution of GHB-lite nearly exact; that is the regime in which GHB-lite was more accurate in the simulations, and it is more accurate here at $\epsilon=10$ and without privacy. And the survival head has 40 coefficients, five times the head of Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga}, so each of GHB's releases is spread over many more directions. On a public benchmark, then, GHB is competitive and always better than a hospital alone with the default prior, the released version is the most accurate method in some of its settings and not in others, and the claims of this paper are limited accordingly.

\subsection{Images: a frozen public encoder}\label{sec:real-images}

Many small clinical problems are images. GHB extends to them without changing its privacy argument, because everything new is public or local. Each image is embedded by a frozen public encoder, FLamby's EfficientNet-B0 with its ImageNet weights \cite{tan2019efficientnet,ogierduterrail2022flamby}, and projected on the first 32 principal components of public unlabeled images; each hospital fits one softmax head over the classes on these features, standardized with its own training statistics and clipped. The network makes one joint release, the secure sum of every patient's score (with a small share of the budget for the class counts that start the iteration), with sensitivity $2\sqrt{2}\,G$ for the softmax; the curvature it needs is computed from the public images, which needs features and the current model but no label, so the information release of Section~\ref{sec:logit} is not needed. Each hospital then forms its leave-one-out prior and integrates the heterogeneity out as before. Every method, GHB and the literature alike, predicts with the logit adjustment of \cite{menon2021longtail} by the class mix that the model implies on the hospital's own training images, restricted to the classes the hospital has seen, a local correction for label shift that costs no privacy.

\paragraph{Development on ISIC 2019} The image version was developed on FLamby's Fed-ISIC2019 \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby,combalia2019bcn20000}, six centers and eight classes of dermoscopic images, declared as development data in advance (\texttt{dev/PREREG\_image.md}); its public images were the ISIC 2019 Test images, which belong to no center. Over \SimIsicImgReps{} repetitions, GHB reached a mean balanced accuracy of \SimIsicTenGHB{} at $\epsilon=10$, against \SimIsicLocal{} for each hospital alone and \SimIsicTenBestReal{} for the best literature method tuned on the development networks (\SimIsicTenBestRealName); at $\epsilon=1$ it was level with a hospital alone (\SimIsicOneGHB). Because every choice was made looking at these numbers, they show what the design does on the data it was made on, not that it generalizes.

\paragraph{Pre-registered confirmation on Camelyon17} The configuration was then frozen and tested on Camelyon17 \cite{bandi2019camelyon17,koh2021wilds}, lymph-node histopathology from five Dutch hospitals with a binary task, tumor or normal tissue in a 96-pixel patch. Its rule, data and five predictions were committed before any Camelyon17 image or label was downloaded. Camelyon17-WILDS could not be downloaded (its host served an expired certificate), so the patches were rebuilt from the organizers' release of the slides with the WILDS preprocessing code; the rebuilt set matches the released one in three centers exactly and within 0.9\% in the other two. To keep the hospitals small, each uses 200 training patches (100 per class, a stratified random split with a fixed seed) and 1,000 test patches from a fixed pool of 1,200; the public images are 2,000 unlabeled patches from ten slides of the challenge's test set, whose patients are not among the hospitals'. The literature methods use the configurations frozen on the development networks of Section~\ref{sec:real-tcga} (realistic arm) or the best on the test patches (oracle), over \SimCamReps{} repetitions. All five predictions held (Table~\ref{tab:images}): at $\epsilon=10$ GHB's balanced accuracy, \SimCamTenGHB, was above a hospital alone (\SimCamLocal, higher in \SimCamTenGHBWinsLocal{} of \SimCamReps{} repetitions) and above every realistic literature method (best \SimCamTenBestReal, \SimCamTenBestRealName); at $\epsilon=1$ it was not below a hospital alone (\SimCamOneGHB), although DP-FedAvg with fine-tuning was slightly above it there (\SimCamOneBestReal); without privacy it was above a hospital alone and the pooled model (\SimCamPooled); and its log-loss at $\epsilon=10$ was lower than local (\SimCamTenGHBLL{} against \SimCamLocalLL). The margins are small, however. The task is easy for a frozen encoder, every reasonable method is near 0.89, and most of GHB's gain over a hospital alone is regularization: the local model with the evidence-weighted prior reaches \SimCamLocalEB. The lead over the realistic arm is one to two paired standard errors, and each literature family tuned on the test patches is above GHB (best \SimCamTenBestOrc). Patches of one slide are correlated and the privacy unit is a patch, not a patient, so the guarantee is weaker than for the tabular networks; this holds for every method compared.

\subsection{What the real data say}\label{sec:real-summary}

Five findings hold across the real networks we report. First, under strong privacy, per-hospital releases carry too little to borrow: GHB-lite, however regularized, does no better than a hospital alone, and a method that releases per-hospital quantities cannot beat that floor by much. Second, releasing only network sums, calibrated to the whole network and read through the hospitals' real information rather than a public feature distribution, is what lets collaboration pay. On the five confirmation networks, GHB was more accurate than every literature method tuned as a consortium could tune it, and than each of them tuned on the test data, at both budgets. Third, the gain grows with what the network can share: it is largest with the richer head and several networks fitted together, smaller on networks of a few hundred patients with a four-coefficient head, where GHB leads every method a consortium could tune at $\epsilon=10$ and is level with a well-regularized hospital at $\epsilon=1$, and mixed on the FLamby benchmark \cite{ogierduterrail2022flamby}, where Ditto and DP-FedAvg with fine-tuning and secure aggregation remain more accurate in some settings. Fourth, GHB is safe on average: in every study and at every budget its mean error was at most that of the hospital alone with the default prior, which several tuned literature methods could not say; single networks can still lose a little, such as the kidney network at $\epsilon=1$ (\SimRFOneVMax). Fifth, base rates differ between real hospitals: the final version, which does not borrow the intercept, improved the mean error in every real study we ran it on, except the deviance on Fed-TCGA-BRCA, and cost accuracy in simulated worlds that share base rates. On images, with a frozen public encoder, the pre-registered predictions on Camelyon17 held, but by small margins over a well-regularized hospital and the literature as a consortium could tune it, and below each literature method tuned on the test patches. The real data show GHB as the most accurate method on the networks it was confirmed on, and as competitive elsewhere, but not as dominating the literature, and the paper does not claim it.
